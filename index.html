This is what \textit{creates} linear motion. \item \textbf{Centre of Mass (CoM):} The point at which the body is balanced in all directions. \item \textbf{Scalar Quantity:} A measurement that has size (magnitude) only. (e.g., Distance, Speed). \item \textbf{Vector Quantity:} A measurement that has both size (magnitude) AND direction. (e.g., Displacement, Velocity, Acceleration). \end{itemize} % Creating Linear Motion \section*{Creating Linear Motion} To generate purely linear motion without any spin (angular motion), a \textbf{direct force} must be applied exactly through the object's \textbf{Centre of Mass}.\\[0.2cm] \textbf{Sporting Example:} A football player taking a penalty kick who wants the ball to travel completely straight with no spin must strike the ball exactly through its centre of mass (a "knuckleball" strike). \begin{center} \begin{tikzpicture} % Football \draw[thick, fill=gray!10] (0,0) circle (1.5); It requires a \textbf{direct force} through the \textbf{centre of mass}. Remember to distinguish carefully between \textbf{scalars} (Distance, Speed) and \textbf{vectors} (Displacement, Velocity, Acceleration) as this dictates which equations you use! \end{tcolorbox} \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=boxorange, colframe=borderorange, arc=4pt, boxrule=1.5pt, title=\textbf{💡 Examiner's Tip}] In the exam, when you are asked to calculate velocity or acceleration, \textbf{always show your working out} and \textbf{never forget the correct units} (e.g., m/s or m/s\textsuperscript{2}).
